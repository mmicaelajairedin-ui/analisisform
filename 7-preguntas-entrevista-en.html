The 7 interview questions you'll always get. (and how to prep them in 48 hours)

8 min read
Back to blog

I've run 800+ interviews as a recruiter. About 90% revolve around the same 7 questions. I'll show you what interviewers are really looking for behind each one and how to answer without sounding scripted.

There's a widespread myth about interviews: that every company has its own unique questions, its strange exercises, its surprise tests. And while there are variations, the truth is that 90% of interviews come down to the same 7 questions, phrased a thousand different ways.

If you prepare these 7 well, you'll walk into almost any process ready.

The point isn't to memorize answers. It's to understand what the recruiter is looking for behind each question. Once you know what they're evaluating, you stop improvising and start communicating exactly what they need to hear.

1. "Tell me about yourself" / "Walk me through your background"

What it looks like: a friendly icebreaker.

What it actually is: the most strategic question in the whole interview.

In 60-90 seconds you're going to set the frame the recruiter uses to read everything you say afterward. If you ramble, if you start at university, if you jump between topics — you lose.

The structure that works:

  • Present (15 seconds): what you do now and where
  • Past (30 seconds): the path that brought you here, choosing only the milestones relevant to the role
  • Future (15 seconds): why this role, right now

Example:

"I'm currently a Senior PM at a B2B SaaS startup, where I lead the product roadmap for our international markets. I came into product from consulting — I spent 4 years at a large firm working with retail clients, and that's where I realized what interested me most was how solutions were built, not how they were sold. So I made the jump into product 3 years ago. Now I'm looking for a next step at a company with global ambition, which is exactly why this role caught my attention."

Ninety seconds. It closes with your reason for being there. The recruiter now has the map for the rest of the interview.

2. "Why do you want to leave your current job?"

What they're evaluating: emotional maturity. If you badmouth your boss or your company, they assume you'll say the same about the next one.

What NOT to say:

  • "My boss is toxic"
  • "They don't value me"
  • "The pay is bad"
  • "The environment is negative"

Even if all of that is true, frame it in the positive.

The formula: talk about what you're moving toward, not what you're running from.

Instead of: "I want out because there's no growth."

Better: "I'm at a point where I want to take on more technical responsibility and work with larger teams, and in my current role that next step isn't available in the near term."

Same idea, radically different energy.

3. "Why are you interested in this role / this company?"

What they're evaluating: whether you did your homework. If you show up with generic answers like "because it's a great company" or "because I like the industry," they assume you're firing off résumés at random.

What actually works:

  • Mention something specific they've done recently (a product they launched, an expansion, an initiative they announced)
  • Connect a concrete skill of yours to a challenge the company has
  • Show you understand where the role sits in the organization (not just the job description)

Example:

"I saw the launch of [product] a few months ago and it stood out to me because it's exactly the kind of problem I worked on in my last role — scaling self-service onboarding in emerging markets. From what I read in the job description, it looks like you're building the team that will take that to the next phase, and I think that's somewhere I can contribute a lot."

You demonstrate: research, context, a concrete value proposition.

4. "Tell me about a professional challenge"

It also shows up as: "Tell me about a difficult project," "A tricky situation you resolved," "A failure you learned from."

What they're evaluating: how you think under pressure. Whether you can analyze a situation. Whether you learn from experience.

Recommended structure (the STAR method):

  • Situation: brief context (15 seconds)
  • Task: your specific responsibility
  • Action: what you did (not the team, you)
  • Result: what happened, with numbers if possible

A trick that works: prepare 3 different stories that cover different kinds of challenges: one technical, one about stakeholders / internal politics, and one about a failure / lesson learned. With those three you can answer almost any variation.

5. "What are your strengths?"

What they're evaluating: self-awareness plus the ability to communicate value.

What doesn't work: listing 5 generic qualities ("I'm proactive, organized, hardworking, a great communicator...").

What does work: pick 1-2 strengths relevant to the role and back them up with a concrete example.

Example:

"My biggest strength is the ability to simplify complex problems for non-technical audiences. In my current role, I run the biweekly leadership presentation where we summarize roadmap and metrics — that meeting used to run 90 minutes and lose focus. I redesigned it into 30 minutes with a fixed format, and since then it's become the main source of decision-making for the executive team."

One strength, one example, one result. Far stronger than saying "I'm a good communicator."

6. "What are your weaknesses?"

Everyone's favorite meme question. And the one people answer worst.

What doesn't work:

  • "I'm too much of a perfectionist" (sounds fake)
  • "I work too hard" (even worse)
  • "I don't have any weaknesses, haha" (an automatic disqualifier)

What does work: pick a real weakness that isn't disqualifying for the role, and show you're working on it.

Example:

"My natural tendency is to jump into problems in solve mode before listen mode. I've worked hard over the last 2 years to slow myself down — to fully understand before proposing. I take notes during meetings so I don't interrupt, and review them afterward. It's still something I have to be conscious about."

Honest, self-aware, with action. It doesn't disqualify you for the role.

Rule: never name a weakness that's core to the role. If you're interviewing for a team management role, don't say "I struggle to delegate." If you're interviewing for a sales role, don't say "I find it hard to talk to strangers."

7. "Do you have any questions for us?"

The most underrated question. And the one that makes the biggest difference.

Saying "no, I think everything's clear" is saying "I'm not really interested."

Questions that show interest and maturity:

  • "How is success measured in this role over the first 6 months?"
  • "What's the biggest challenge the team is facing right now?"
  • "What qualities did the people who've thrived in this role tend to have?"
  • "How would you describe the team culture? What makes it different from other companies?"
  • "What happened with the person who held this role before? Internal promotion, or did they leave the company?"

Questions you should NOT ask in a first interview:

  • Salary (leave it for HR / later rounds, unless they raise it first)
  • Vacation days, hours, benefits
  • "When will I hear back?" (the classic that signals anxiety)

The 48-hour prep

If you have an interview in 2 days, this is the order:

Day 1 (90 minutes):

  • Reread the job description, underline the 5 key requirements
  • Research the company: website, LinkedIn, latest news (15 min)
  • Look up the interviewer on LinkedIn (5 min)
  • Prepare your 3 STAR stories

Day 2 (60 minutes):

  • Rehearse the 7 answers out loud (not in your head — out loud)
  • Prepare the 3-4 questions to ask at the end
  • Test your technical setup if it's a video call

1 hour before:

  • Reread your résumé (yes, your own) — and if it isn't polished yet, generate an ATS-optimized one with AI
  • Reread the job description one more time
  • Breathe. You're not begging for a job. You're having a professional conversation to see whether it's a fit.

One final observation

The people who interview best aren't the ones who prepare their answers the most. They're the ones who understand that an interview is a conversation between two people deciding whether they want to work together.

You're evaluating too. You're deciding too. You're not in an inferior position.

When you walk in with that energy, everything changes.
